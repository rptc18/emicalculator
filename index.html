How Equated Monthly Installments (EMI) Work

An Equated Monthly Installment (EMI) is a fixed payment amount made by a borrower to a lender at a specified date each calendar month. EMIs are applied to both interest and principal each month so that over a specified number of years, the loan is fully paid off.

EMI = [P x R x (1+R)^N] / [(1+R)^N - 1]

Where:
• P = Principal loan amount requested
• R = Monthly interest rate (Annual Interest Rate divided by 12 months divided by 100)
• N = Total number of monthly installments (Loan tenure in years multiplied by 12)

How EMI is Calculated: Deconstructing the Math

When you secure a loan from a bank or financial institution, your repayment is structured into Equated Monthly Installments (EMIs). While the monthly payout remains identical, the allocation of that money toward interest versus principal undergoes continuous change.

The Reducing Balance Method

Most commercial loans run on a reducing balance basis. Under this model, interest for the current month is calculated exclusively on the outstanding balance at that moment. During early loan cycles, because the outstanding balance is high, the interest portion forms the largest segment of your payment. As principal balance diminishes, monthly interest drops proportionally, allowing a larger slice of your payment to pay down principal.

Mathematical Proof

Understanding this math allows borrowers to see why making extra prepayments during early loan years delivers maximum interest savings.

Reduce Your EMI Burden: Proven Strategies

High monthly installment payments can strain household budgets. Implementing structured strategies can drastically cut overall interest costs and speed up debt-free milestones.

1. Execute Strategic Partial Prepayments

Allocating annual employment bonuses or tax refunds toward partial principal prepayments lowers your total outstanding loan balance immediately. Because subsequent interest calculations use the smaller balance, your overall tenure drops significantly.

2. Refinance via Balance Transfer

If market interest rates fall or your credit score improves significantly, explore transferring your high-interest balance to another lender offering lower annual rates.

Fixed vs. Floating Interest Rates: Making the Right Choice

One of the vital decisions when finalizing a loan agreement is choosing between fixed and floating interest rate structures.

Fixed Interest Rates

Fixed rate loans lock in your interest rate for the entire tenure or a designated period. This delivers complete payment predictability and shields your household budget from central bank rate hikes.

Floating Interest Rates

Floating rate loans move dynamically with external financial market benchmarks (such as repo rates). When interest rate cycles drop, your overall interest cost automatically declines without requiring loan refinancing.
